The decision

Five courses of action, the recommended sequence, and the tax arithmetic that separates them.

Renew if the tenant will commit. Never sell for cash. Route any exit through a §1031 exchange.

On risk-adjusted after-tax wealth the ranking is stable across 6,000 simulated macro paths: renew-and-hold-with-a-deferred-exit first, a §1031 exchange executed now second, and an outright cash sale last. The outright cash sale is dominated in every scenario tested, and the reason is tax, not the market.

Recommended sequence

These six steps are ordered. Each one is conditional on the outcome of the one before it, and the first two should happen immediately regardless of what is decided about the exit.

1

Do not sell for cash. In this fact pattern it is dominated.

On a $6.5 million asset with a 40% adjusted basis, a cash sale nets about $4.83 million after brokerage, Los Angeles transfer tax and capital gains — a 25.7% haircut. A §1031 exchange of the identical asset on the identical day nets about $5.94 million, because the capital gains tax of roughly $1.12 million is deferred rather than paid. Roughly 17% of asset value is destroyed for no economic reason other than liquidity. If a sale happens, it must be structured.

2

Separate the exit decision from the structure decision.

These are two different decisions and they are routinely conflated. Should I exit? is a question about the tenant, the lease term and concentration risk. How do I exit? is a question about tax. The model says the second decision is worth more than the first: on the base case, changing only the structure of a sale changes terminal after-tax wealth by more than the entire difference between selling in 2026 and selling in 2035.

3

Open renewal discussions now, while the tenant is improving.

A renewal is worth more than the exit it precedes, because it removes the short-lease cap-rate penalty (A16) and converts a decaying asset back into a long-dated income stream. Approach the tenant's real-estate group while same-store sales are recovering — from −7.4% to −1.1% (S12, S13) — because that is when a franchisee will commit capital and a landlord has leverage. Do it well before any contractual renewal notice deadline, which is currently unknown (F-01).

4

If the tenant commits: renew, then exchange out on a planned horizon.

This is the highest risk-adjusted outcome in the model (strategy S7, and the best strategy at risk aversion γ=4). The point of renewing is not to hold forever. It is to be holding a long-lease, low-friction asset at the moment you choose to exchange it, because a box with ten years of term trades to a different buyer pool at a tighter cap rate than the same box with four years left.

5

If the tenant will not commit: exchange out before the term erodes.

If renewal is refused, the asset is on a clock and the clock is priced. Sell into a §1031 while four to six years of term remain, not after. The cost of waiting is visible in the exit scan: cumulative exposure to at least one shock rises from 11.4% at a 2032 exit to 16.6% at 2035, and the cap-rate penalty widens every year in between. Do not wait for certainty. The market charges for it.

6

Use the exchange to fix the concentration, not to replicate it.

The real risk in this file is not the tenant, the cap rate or the wildfires. It is that one individual's wealth is concentrated in one box, in one county, whose population is projected to fall by 240,800 school students over ten years (S09) and which has just absorbed the largest urban fire loss in its history (S21, S22). A §1031 into a diversified set of net-lease assets, or into a professionally managed Delaware Statutory Trust, keeps the deferral and removes the single-asset, single-metro bet. If the owner will not diversify, the case for exiting earlier gets materially stronger.

Risk attached to each decision available to you

Five courses of action are actually open. Each is assessed on what it protects and what it costs, in both directions.

Sell now, for cash

Upside
Certainty. Liquidity. Removes tenant, wildfire, seismic and regulatory exposure in one transaction.
Downside
Surrenders roughly a third of accumulated appreciation to tax. Pays Measure ULA if the price exceeds $5.4 million — a cliff, so a $5.45 million sale nets less than a $5.39 million sale. Gives up the income stream with no replacement asset, so the proceeds must be redeployed into a fully taxed environment.
Assessment
Dominated. Ranked last or near-last on risk-adjusted wealth in every scenario tested.

Sell now, via §1031 exchange

Upside
Defers the entire capital gain, removes single-asset and single-metro concentration, and has the lowest dispersion of outcomes of any strategy modelled. Certainty of execution is high.
Downside
Requires identifying replacement property inside tight statutory deadlines, and the replacement must be bought competitively in the same market. Deferral is not elimination — the tax follows the replacement asset. Transaction costs are paid twice.
Assessment
Strong. Best outcome at high risk aversion among the immediate-exit strategies.

Hold to lease expiry and decide then

Upside
Keeps every option open and pays nothing today.
Downside
The worst of both worlds: the cap-rate penalty (A16) widens as term runs down, so the asset is worth less each year for a reason unrelated to the market. Risk of a shock accumulates with time held — 11.4% cumulative by 2032 and 16.6% by 2035 on assumption A13. Deciding late means deciding with less leverage.
Assessment
Avoid. Dominated by deciding now in either direction.

Renew, then hold long

Upside
Highest median outcome. Captures rent growth above contract escalation (A04 versus A03), removes the short-lease cap-rate penalty, and defers all tax for as long as the asset is held.
Downside
Concentrates wealth further into one asset in a county losing population. Extends exposure to a hazard class that produced two catastrophic events in one week in 2025. Liquidity falls to zero for the hold period. Requires the tenant to remain solvent for fifteen-plus years.
Assessment
Best on median and at low risk aversion. Best overall once paired with a deferred exit.

Renew, then exchange out on a planned horizon

Upside
Takes the renewal gain and the deferral gain together. Highest risk-adjusted outcome in the model (strategy S7, best at risk aversion γ=4). Converts a decaying short-lease asset into a saleable long-lease asset before selling it.
Downside
Depends on the tenant actually renewing (A05 = 62%). Requires active management of both the lease negotiation and the exchange calendar. Two things must go right instead of one.
Assessment
Recommended, conditional on a tenant renewal commitment.

The ranking flips with risk tolerance, and that flip is the whole finding. At low risk aversion the long hold wins on the median. At high risk aversion the renew-then-exchange route wins, because it keeps most of the median while truncating the tail. The one result that never changes: a cash sale is never the right answer.

Why the cash sale cannot win

The gap is almost entirely tax. Take the $6.5 million band, a 40% adjusted basis (A10) and a 33.4% blended rate on gain (A11).

  1. Transfer tax. Below the threshold, county plus city is 0.56% — about $36,400 on $6.5 million. Above $5.4 million, Measure ULA adds 4% of the entire price: $260,000. Total $296,400, an effective 4.56%.
  2. Selling costs. Brokerage, title, escrow and legal at 4.0% (A09): $260,000.
  3. Capital gains. Net of the above, the gain over a $2.6 million basis is about $3.34 million, taxed at 33.4%: roughly $1.12 million.
  4. Total friction. About $1.67 million, or 25.7% of asset value, before a dollar is reinvested. A §1031 exchange defers item 3 entirely.
  5. The compounding cost. That deferred tax then compounds for the whole hold period. That is why the terminal-wealth gap is far larger than the tax bill.

None of this argues for holding forever. It argues that the exit decision and the structure decision are separate, and the structure decision is worth more.

What would change this recommendation

  • The tenant refuses to renew. Then the recommendation becomes step 4: exchange out before term erodes, not after. In the model, exchanging into a renewal and then out in 2032 rather than holding to expiry preserves about $4.5 million of terminal wealth on a $6.5 million asset.
  • The property is already worth more than $10.9 million. Then the upper ULA cliff applies at 5.5% and the case for exchanging rather than selling gets stronger, not weaker.
  • The owner has a very low basis and needs liquidity. Even then, a §1031 into a diversified trust achieves liquidity through a later sale of units at a tighter tax cost than a direct asset sale. The sensitivity table shows the answer is not sensitive to basis within the tested range.
  • The owner will not diversify. Then renewing concentrates the entire estate into one box in one county that is losing population and just absorbed the largest urban fire loss in its history. The case for an early exchange gets materially stronger.
