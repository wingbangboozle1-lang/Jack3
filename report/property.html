The asset

A net lease transfers the costs. It does not transfer the risk.

The anonymised asset, the NNN structure, what the tenant's filings disclose about its leases, and the fifteen fields that would turn this into a valuation.

The subject is anonymised, deliberately

This is Asset A: a single-tenant quick-service restaurant property under a triple-net lease, in a Central Los Angeles submarket, with a primary term expiring somewhere between 2030 and 2035. That is the entire verified description. There is no address, parcel number, building size, rent, basis or debt schedule in this file, and none will be added. The privacy directive is the reason, and it is enforced by a build check.

The lease structure, as a structure

Under a true NNN lease the tenant pays taxes, insurance and maintenance, so the owner's operating exposure is close to zero while the lease runs. That is often described as passive ownership. It is not: it transfers every operating cost to the tenant and leaves the owner with 100% of the continuation risk.

The one inference that matters

A disclosed initial term of generally 20 years, running to a 2030-2035 expiry, implies a lease that began around 2010-2015 — consistent with a mature site. The issuer's renewal options are disclosed as generally exercisable at the company's sole discretion. If the owner's lease mirrors that, the renewal decision belongs to the tenant, and the entire 'renew' branch is not the owner's to take.

Why the model had to be normalised

Because the rent was never supplied, the projection model is written in units of R = one year of the current contractual base rent. Nothing is invented, and the ranking still works: a sell-versus-hold comparison is two cash-flow streams from the same asset, so the rent level largely cancels out. The owner multiplies by their own rent in private.
