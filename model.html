Break-even calculation

Analysis · disclosed arithmetic, not a forecast

What the public record can answer, and what it cannot

Cannot: a sale price, a market rent, a cap rate, or the chance the tenant renews. No government agency publishes a cap rate for this asset, the tenant's lease is private, and the owner has not supplied rent, basis or debt. Anything presented as a dollar forecast would therefore be invented.

Can: the one comparison the assumed inputs decide. Restate the question in units that do not need the missing facts: with a required return, an exit cap rate and a rent-growth rate, is 10 more years of rent plus a sale at the end of them worth more than selling today at today's cap rate? Every money figure below is a multiple of one year of current contractual base rent, which cancels out of the ranking.

Disclosed assumptions. Horizon 10 years (to 2035, the latest expiry you reported); rent paid in arrears and growing annually; exit at the end of year 10 at the stated cap applied to then-current rent; base cap rate today 5.5% for every comparison; discount-rate floor 4.86% from the 10-year Treasury on 2026-11-06 (Federal Reserve H.15 via FRED DGS10), with higher required returns swept because the owner's is private.

Excluded and disclosed: transaction costs, the 0.45% city base transfer tax, Measure ULA at 4%/5.5%, federal and California income tax, depreciation recapture at up to 25%, capital expenditure, insurance repricing, financing, and tenant default before expiry. Each is a verified rule or a private fact; apply it on top of these multiples.

Read down a column: the more return you require, the more rent growth the hold has to deliver merely to break even. At a 6.0% exit cap, a 4.86% required return needs about +0.03% a year of rent growth; at a 7.0% required return the same asset has to grow rent at about +2.19% a year. Nothing in the published regional data establishes either rate, which is why the answer turns on your required return and on the exit cap, not on county demographics.

The stress column is the asymmetry that decides the risk balance: an 18-month void at expiry costs roughly a tenth of the holding value, and it arrives at the moment the lease gives you no protection. Selling while the income is contractual converts an unknown renewal into a priced outcome, which is why the recommendation runs the sale track and the renewal track at the same time rather than waiting for the option clock.
